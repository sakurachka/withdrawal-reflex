Why do hot things hurt?

Touch something hot and the signal starts in a nociceptor — a bare nerve ending threaded into your skin — where heat-sensitive ion channels convert that heat into an electrical current.

Receptor potential → action potential → firing rate

The Na⁺ current from above doesn't fire a spike directly — it first builds a graded receptor (generator) potential: the slow climbing part of the trace, proportional to how much current is flowing. Only when that climb crosses threshold do fast voltage-gated Na⁺ channels — a different protein entirely — flip open and fire one all-or-none action potential, the sharp spike. Count spikes per second and you get the firing rate: how temperature is actually encoded, not the spike's size (every spike looks identical).

For the curious: the actual model underneath
Step 1 — heat changes a protein's shape. TRPV1 and TRPV2 sit side by side in the nerve ending's membrane. TRPV1 opens starting around 43°C — the half-activation point measured by Caterina et al. (1997) — and TRPV2 only once it's genuinely extreme, around 52°C. Each follows its own S-shaped (Boltzmann) curve: a probability of being open at any instant, not a switch — that's what the sliding purple gates represent.

Step 2 — the open channel is where "electrical" actually starts. Once open, the pore mostly passes Na⁺. Sodium is concentrated outside the cell and the inside sits at a negative resting voltage, so Na⁺ is pulled in down its electrochemical gradient the instant the gate opens — drawn as the falling ion in the diagram. That inward trickle of positive charge is the current: I_total = I_TRPV1(T) + I_TRPV2(T). Below 43°C neither channel contributes; between 43°C and 52°C only TRPV1 is active; above 52°C both are open at once, so the current climbs faster.

Step 3 — current builds a graded receptor potential. That Na⁺ current depolarizes the membrane locally, proportional to how much is flowing — the slow climbing part of the trace above. This is not a spike yet; it's called a receptor (or generator) potential, and it can be small or large, fast or slow, matching the stimulus continuously.

Step 4 — threshold triggers a completely different channel. If that climb depolarizes the nerve's spike-initiation zone past roughly −55 mV, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels — unrelated proteins to TRPV1/2 — flip open in a self-reinforcing cascade, firing one full, all-or-none action potential: the sharp overshoot in the trace (a real spike reaches roughly +30 mV) before the membrane resets and starts climbing again.

Step 5 — the rate is the message. Our leaky integrate-and-fire model compresses steps 3–4 into one climb-and-reset equation, which is why the trace's rise looks smooth right up to a sudden spike. Every action potential looks identical; only how often they fire — the rate — carries the information about how hot it actually is. This page deliberately stops there — it doesn't model fiber types, the spinal cord, or a motor reflex.

TRPV1 — early-warning heat channel

The channel's natural switch-on point. Inflammatory mediators (bradykinin, prostaglandins) released by damaged tissue chemically tag TRPV1 and drag this down — sometimes below body temperature, which is why a warm shower stings sunburned skin, or why in chronic pain ordinary warmth starts to hurt (allodynia).

How co-operatively the channel's subunits snap open together. Higher reads as a hair-trigger; lower feels like a slow-building ache rather than a sudden sting.

How many TRPV1 channels sit in the membrane. Nerve growth factor and chronic inflammation drive the cell to make more of them, raising this — part of why healed-but-inflamed tissue stays hyper-sensitive to heat generally, not just at a lower threshold.

TRPV2 — extreme-heat channel

TRPV2's own switch-on point. It's far less plastic than TRPV1 — inflammation barely shifts it — which is consistent with its job as a fixed, last-resort alarm for tissue-damaging heat.

Same idea as TRPV1's sharpness, for the high-heat channel.

Channel density for TRPV2. Changes less in disease than TRPV1's does — included mainly so you can see both currents add together above 52°C.

Neuron integration

How fast charge builds and leaks at the nerve ending itself, once current is already flowing. Cooling a nerve — an ice pack, a cold compress — slows channel and membrane kinetics directly, which is part of why cold numbs pain.
