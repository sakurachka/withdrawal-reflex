Before you start: nociception is not pain

This explainer is about nociceptionThe nervous system detecting and signalling potentially harmful stimuli. It can drive a reflex without any feeling of pain.: your nervous system detecting and signalling harm. Pain is a perception, and it needs the brain. Your hand can start pulling away before you feel anything.

    Step 2 · Transmission

    How does the signal reach your spine, and still say where the heat is?

    Dotted underlined words have a short definition. Hover over one to read it.

    From fingertip to spine

    In the previous step you met the nociceptors: nerve endings in your skin that respond to heat. Now, how does their signal get from your fingertip to your spine?

    The answer is b. Nociceptors are very long: a single nerve fibreThe long, thin extension of a nerve cell (its axon) that carries the electrical signal. runs from the tip of your finger through the whole length of your arm. Its cell body sits off to the side, in the dorsal root ganglionA cluster of sensory nerve cell bodies just outside the spinal cord. The signal passes by them without stopping. next to the spine.

    Saved. We'll come back to it.

    There are no synapsesThe junction where one nerve cell passes its signal to the next. Each one adds a small delay. on the way, so there are no relay points to add delay. The travel time is simply the time it takes the fibre to conduct the signal.

    The modeltravel time = distance ÷ speed

    Where the equations come from

    1. travel time = distance ÷ speed Holds because one nociceptor runs the whole way with no synapses, so there is no relay delay. Speeds for pain fibres: Aδ about 5–30 m/s, C about 0.4–1.4 m/s (Dubin & Patapoutian, 2010). The 15 m/s and 1 m/s used here are values inside those ranges.
    2. myelinated: speed ≈ 6 × total diameter (m/s per µm) Measured on cat nerve fibres by Hursh (1939). Rushton (1951) showed why theory predicts speed to grow in proportion to diameter.
    3. efficiency ∝ g · √(−ln g), with g = axon ÷ total diameter From Rushton's (1951) theory of myelinated fibres. It peaks at g = e−1/2 ≈ 0.6. Here it is scaled so that g = 0.6 gives a factor of 1.
    4. unmyelinated: speed ∝ √diameter The classic cable-theory result for bare fibres (Hodgkin, 1954). The constant (1 m/s at 1 µm) is our own choice, set so a typical C fibre lands near 1 m/s.

    These are teaching approximations. Real fibres vary, and measured C-fibre speeds do not follow the square-root rule exactly.

    Where is the heat?

    Every action potential looks the same, so the pulse itself can't say "left index finger". Location comes from which fibre is firing: each fibre only listens to its own patch of skin, its receptive fieldThe patch of skin that one sensory fibre responds to., and enters the spinal cord at a specific segmentA level of the spinal cord. Each one receives nerves from one band of the body. Fingertips feed segments C6 to C8 in the neck..

    How the explainer would put it

    1. Transduction. Heat opens channels such as TRPV1 in nerve endings in your skin. Charged ions flow in and the nerve fires.
    2. Transmission. One long nerve fibre carries the signal from your fingertip to your spinal cord, with no relay on the way. The fast fibre gets there in about a twentieth of a second, and says where the heat is.
    3. Spinal integration. In the spinal cord, interneurons pass the signal straight on to motor neurons, and a copy goes up to the brain.
    4. Motor output. Motor neurons release acetylcholine onto the arm muscles, which contract and pull your hand away.

    So the brain doesn't decide. The circuit in your spinal cord pulls your hand back, and the feeling of pain arrives in your brain a moment later. Compare this with what you wrote above: what would you add or change?
